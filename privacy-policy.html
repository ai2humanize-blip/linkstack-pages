Privacy Policy

What a Privacy Policy Is

A privacy policy is a public explanation of how an organization handles personal information. It describes the information the organization collects, why it collects that information, how it is used and shared, and the choices available to individuals. Depending on the organization’s location, services, audience, and data practices, a privacy policy may also be required by law. Its purpose is to provide transparency, not merely to satisfy a legal formality.

Why a Privacy Policy Matters

A clear policy helps people understand what to expect before providing information. It also reduces confusion about data sharing, marketing, cookies, account access, and data retention. Internally, the policy can guide employees and contractors toward consistent data practices. Because privacy expectations and legal requirements change, a policy should be treated as a maintained business document rather than a page written once and never reviewed.

What a Complete Privacy Policy Should Explain

The appropriate sections will vary, but a comprehensive policy commonly addresses the following:

  • Scope and identity: State the organization’s legal name, the services and websites covered, and any affiliated organizations or third parties included in the explanation.
  • Information collected: Describe the categories of data involved, such as account details, contact information, transaction records, device identifiers, communications, and information submitted through forms.
  • Sources of information: Explain whether data comes directly from individuals, from service providers, from integrations, or automatically through technologies such as cookies and similar tools.
  • Purposes and legal bases: Explain why each category of information is used. Where relevant, identify the legal basis for processing, such as consent, performance of a contract, legal obligations, or legitimate interests.
  • How information is shared: List the circumstances in which data may be disclosed, including to processors, business partners, professional advisers, authorities, or other parties in a corporate transaction.
  • Storage, security, and retention: Describe general safeguards and how long information is kept. Avoid promising that data can never be breached or misused.
  • Individual rights: Explain available rights, such as access, correction, deletion, restriction, objection, portability, or withdrawal of consent, along with the process for making a request.
  • Cookies and tracking: Explain what these technologies do, when they are used, and how users can manage them through available settings or tools.
  • Children and international transfers: Address age-related policies and explain how information may be processed in another country, where that is relevant.
  • Contact and complaint information: Provide a working contact method and, where required, explain how to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority.

Privacy Policy, Privacy Notice, and Consent

These terms are sometimes used interchangeably, but they can have different functions. A privacy notice explains data processing in a concise way. A privacy policy is often the broader published document containing the complete explanation. Consent is a separate mechanism for obtaining permission when it is legally required or otherwise appropriate. Publishing a policy does not automatically establish consent, and consent to one activity should not be presented as permission for unrelated processing.

How to Write a Useful Policy

Start with an inventory of the organization’s actual data practices. Verify which tools collect information, which vendors receive data, how long records are retained, and which rights workflows exist. Then write in plain, direct language. Define specialized terms, use descriptive headings, and place important information near the beginning. Strong presentation is not a substitute for completeness.

The policy should also align with reality. If the website advertises data deletion, the process should work. If the organization claims not to sell personal information, its practices, contracts, and disclosures should support that statement. Conflicts between the policy, consent screens, cookie controls, contracts, and actual behavior can create legal and trust problems.

When to Review and Update It

Review the policy when launching a new service, introducing analytics or advertising technology, changing vendors, altering data purposes, expanding into a new jurisdiction, or materially changing security and retention practices. Material changes should be communicated clearly and, where necessary, presented with fresh consent. The revision date and a brief change log can help readers understand whether the document is current.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Using vague language: Phrases such as “we may share information with partners” need meaningful detail.
  • Overstating security: Describe reasonable safeguards without promising absolute protection.
  • Hiding important terms: Do not rely on links to bury disclosures about sensitive processing.
  • Confusing required and optional data: Explain the practical consequence of declining optional collection.
  • Copying another organization’s policy: Tailor every statement to verified practices and operations.
  • Failing to provide a practical route for questions: Give individuals a monitored method for contacting the privacy team.

A Final Quality Check

Before publishing, ask whether a reasonable person can understand what data is collected, why it is collected, who receives it, and what choices they have. Have legal counsel review the document for the jurisdictions and activities involved, but do not substitute legal review for operational accuracy. The strongest privacy policy is one that reflects real, carefully governed practices and is maintained as those practices evolve.
