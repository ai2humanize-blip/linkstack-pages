Anna Nicole Smith’s Husbands: A Clear Relationship Timeline

Anna Nicole Smith, the model and television personality, was married twice. Her final husband was J. P. “Johnny” Hutto, a NASCAR driver and pilot. Her first husband was Michael Ivey, an Air Force pilot. Because many profiles also mention Howard H. “Slick” Leonard, it is easy to confuse a spouse with a close companion. The factual distinction is straightforward: Michael Ivey and Johnny Hutto were Smith’s husbands; Slick Leonard was not.

Michael Ivey: Her First Husband

Smith married Michael Ivey in Las Vegas in 1992. Their son, Nicholas “Prince” Ivey, was born in 1993. The couple separated soon afterward, and their marriage ended in 1995. Biographies and court reporting describe the legal ending as an annulment rather than a conventional divorce.

Prince remained an important part of Smith’s life and was publicly identified as her son with Ivey. The first marriage is also the origin of the surname Ivey, which appears in references to Smith’s family. Although Smith did not live with Prince full-time for much of his childhood, she was known to maintain a relationship with him and provide for him.

Johnny Hutto: Her Second and Final Husband

Smith met Johnny Hutto in the early 2000s, and their relationship later became public. They married in Texas in May 2006. Hutto competed in NASCAR and was also a licensed pilot. At the time of their marriage, he was frequently described as Smith’s longtime boyfriend and eventual husband.

Tragically, Hutto was killed in an aircraft crash in Texas in September 2008, approximately seven months after Smith’s death in February 2008. They did not have children together. Hutto was therefore her second and final legal husband.

What Was Howard H. “Slick” Leonard’s Role?

Howard H. “Slick” Leonard was a longtime friend, bodyguard, and companion of Anna Nicole Smith. Some articles describe him as her boyfriend, while others emphasize his protective role and friendship. He was not married to Smith. This distinction matters when reviewing older biographies or entertainment coverage, where the words “husband,” “boyfriend,” and “companion” are sometimes used inconsistently.
