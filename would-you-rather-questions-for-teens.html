Would You Rather Questions for Teens

Would you rather questions are an easy way to start a conversation, pass time with friends, or get to know someone better. They work especially well with teens because each question invites a personal choice rather than a simple yes-or-no answer. The best questions are imaginative, lighthearted, and open to more than one reasonable answer.

These questions work for classroom icebreakers, family gatherings, road trips, sleepovers, clubs, team events, and one-on-one conversations. No special supplies are needed. You can ask them in sequence, let each person answer, or turn the activity into a playful debate.

Fun Would You Rather Questions for Teens

  • Would you rather be able to pause time for ten seconds or rewind time by ten seconds?
  • Would you rather have an unlimited supply of snacks or an unlimited supply of drinks?
  • Would you rather live in a house in the mountains or a house beside the ocean?
  • Would you rather travel only by train or travel only by airplane?
  • Would you rather have no internet at home or no television?
  • Would you rather know every animal’s language or every plant’s language?
  • Would you rather spend a weekend camping or stay in a luxury hotel?
  • Would you rather be able to talk to animals or communicate with plants?
  • Would you rather wake up early every day or stay up late every night?
  • Would you rather cook every meal or bake every meal?
  • Would you rather have a giant library or a giant game center?
  • Would you rather visit the future or visit the past?
  • Would you rather be a famous musician or a famous inventor?
  • Would you rather make every device wireless or make every device invisible?
  • Would you rather live on Mars or live beneath the ocean?
  • Would you rather have perfect weather or perfect music?
  • Would you rather explore a rainforest or explore outer space?
  • Would you rather lose your phone for a week or lose your favorite game for a week?
  • Would you rather have a personal jet or a personal submarine?
  • Would you rather start every day with a surprise party or end every day with a surprise party?

Thoughtful and Character-Building Questions

Use questions that reveal interests, values, and personality when you want a deeper conversation. These are often more meaningful than questions centered on money, appearance, or physical ability.

  • Would you rather be remembered for your creativity or your kindness?
  • Would you rather lead a group project or work independently?
  • Would you rather have more free time or more money to decide how to spend it?
  • Would you rather live in a busy city or a quiet countryside?
  • Would you rather be excellent at starting things or excellent at finishing things?
  • Would you rather spend a year reading or a year traveling?
  • Would you rather teach a skill or learn a new one?
  • Would you rather have a close group of friends or a wide circle of acquaintances?
  • Would you rather make a difference through art, science, sports, or volunteering?
  • Would you rather have a mentor who gives advice or a partner who works alongside you?
  • Would you rather change one thing about the past or one thing about the future?
  • Would you rather be very good at one sport or average at many?
  • Would you rather protect your privacy completely or share your ideas publicly?
  • Would you rather have a day without plans or a day filled with activities?
  • Would you rather solve a problem alone first or ask for help right away?

How to Make the Game More Interesting

Give each person a chance to answer before anyone changes their choice. If one option seems obviously better, add a twist: both choices could have advantages and drawbacks. For example, “Would you rather have instant answers or instant travel?” encourages people to explain what they value.

You can also assign points for the most creative answer, the most thoughtful reason, or the answer that surprises the group. Another option is to let each person create a new question. If someone gives an answer that reveals an interest, ask a respectful follow-up such as, “What got you interested in that?” This turns a quick game into a real conversation.

For a group activity, write questions on separate pieces of paper and let participants choose one at random. You might separate the papers into funny, imaginative, and thoughtful categories so the mood can change throughout the session.

Tips for Keeping the Conversation Inclusive

Avoid questions that force teens to reveal private information, compare family situations, or choose based on body size. Steer away from prompts that involve dangerous stunts, illegal behavior, or ignoring important safety rules. A “would you rather” question should remain hypothetical and comfortable for everyone present.

It also helps to avoid treating one answer as automatically correct. A choice that seems unusual may make sense for that person. Listen without mocking, ask permission before turning an answer into a story about the person, and make space for quieter participants. If someone does not want to answer, let them skip the question or offer an imaginary response.

The simplest way to keep things flowing is to mix light questions with thoughtful ones. Start with something playful, slow down when the group becomes interested, and follow up on meaningful answers. Used this way, would you rather questions for teens are not just a boredom breaker—they can build trust, encourage creativity, and help people learn what their friends and classmates value.
